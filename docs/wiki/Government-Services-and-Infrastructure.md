# Government, Services & Infrastructure

[← Wiki Home](Home.md)

## Current civic baseline

**Status: Playable and Transitional.**

Current systems include fire, police, healthcare, education, waste, municipal budgets, routed service vehicles, incidents, neighborhood quality, treasury, taxes, and utility capacity.

## Civic Institutions 2.0 target

Public facilities should become operating institutions rather than icons that automatically provide service. A mature facility can own physical capacity, staffing requirements, current staffing, equipment/fleet, operating budget, queues, quality, catchment/accessibility, and maintenance/condition.

Examples include hospital beds and treatment queues; school teachers, classrooms, enrollment, and class size; police/fire/EMS dispatch chains; and waste collection/transfer/treatment logistics.

A physical service unit must not be double-booked.

## Metropolitan infrastructure networks

Future explicit graphs are planned for:

- electricity: generation → transmission → substation → distribution → load;
- water: source → treatment → storage/pumping → distribution;
- wastewater: collection → pumping → treatment → discharge/reuse;
- drainage: catchment → inlet → pipe/channel → detention/outfall;
- telecommunications: backbone → exchange/node → local distribution/coverage.

Network assets should have capacity, condition, maintenance, failures, and upgrade state. Disconnected infrastructure must not provide capacity.

## Municipal finance target

The mature system should include operating/capital budgets, fund accounting, property assessments, taxes, fees, grants, bonds, debt service, credit quality, pensions/long-term liabilities, maintenance backlogs, and capital planning.

A city can appear cash-positive while accumulating unsustainable obligations, so finance should expose both flows and long-run commitments.

## Environment, politics, and planning

Future phases target air/water quality, noise, heat, energy, emissions, rainfall/drought/flood/wildfire exposure, resilience investment, elections, council districts, approval, neighborhood organizations, project coalitions, zoning-code reform, parking rules, historic/environmental overlays, impact fees, inclusionary rules, density bonuses, and other planning-law tools.

These systems should be driven by actual physical, economic, service, fiscal, and resident conditions rather than independent sliders.
